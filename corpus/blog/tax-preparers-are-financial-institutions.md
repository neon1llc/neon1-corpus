# Tax preparers are financial institutions, as far as the FTC is concerned

Once a year, people sit across from you and hand over their whole financial life. Social Security numbers for themselves, their spouse, and their kids. What they earned, what they owe, and the bank account their refund will land in. Most of them never think twice about it, because they trust you. That trust is the business.

It's also why criminals want what's in your files. With one client folder, someone can file a fake return before your client does and take the refund. Your client won't learn about it from the thief. They'll learn about it from the IRS, when their real return gets rejected. In its 2025 report to Congress, the National Taxpayer Advocate described identity theft victims waiting an average of more than 21 months for the IRS to resolve their cases. That's nearly two years of a family waiting on money they were counting on.

The federal government's answer to that risk reaches into your office, whether you work alone at a kitchen table or run a ten-person firm. The Federal Trade Commission names tax preparation services as a "financial institution" under its Safeguards Rule, and it expects you to protect client information the way that rule lays out. Here's what it asks, in plain words, and what you can get done before tax season starts in late January.

## Why the rule reaches tax offices

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act requires financial institutions to protect their customers' personal information. Banks answer to their own regulators. For most other businesses that offer financial services to consumers, tax preparers included, the FTC enforces it through the Safeguards Rule, 16 CFR Part 314.

The FTC rewrote the rule in 2021 to spell out specific requirements, and most of them took effect on June 9, 2023. A later amendment added a duty to report certain breaches to the FTC, starting May 13, 2024. If the rule feels new to you, that's because, in its current form, it mostly is.

## What the rule asks for

The rule asks for a written information security program that fits your size and the sensitivity of what you hold. In the tax world it's usually called a WISP, a written information security plan. For a small office, it comes down to these pieces:

- **One person in charge.** The rule calls this person the Qualified Individual. It can be you, someone on staff, or an outside provider. If it's a provider, you still own the result, and you name someone senior to oversee them.
- **Multi-factor authentication** for anyone who signs in to a system holding client information: email, tax software, the client portal, cloud storage, and remote access. All of them, since a thief only needs the one you skipped.
- **Encryption** of client information when it travels outside your network and when it's stored, including on laptops and backup drives.
- **Access limited to what each job needs**, and a list of the systems, devices, and people that touch client data.
- **Security training for everyone**, kept current as new tricks show up.
- **Contracts that require your service providers to protect the data**, and a periodic check that they do. Your IT person, your software vendors, and any outside bookkeeper all count.
- **Secure disposal** of a former client's information no later than two years after you last used it, unless you have a legal or business reason to keep it.
- **A written plan for what happens when something goes wrong**: who decides, who gets told, and in what order.

If you keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, you can skip four of the formal pieces: the written risk assessment, penetration testing and scans, the written incident response plan, and the annual report to leadership. Everything else still applies, including MFA, encryption, and training. Count carefully before you lean on this. Former clients count, and an office that has been open a while usually holds more people's information than its current client list suggests.

## The 30-day clock

Since May 13, 2024, if unencrypted information on 500 or more people is taken, you have to tell the FTC through a form on its website as soon as possible, and no later than 30 days after you find out. Encrypted data counts as unencrypted if the key was taken too. The FTC can publish these reports. New York's own breach notification law applies on top of that, with notices to the people affected and to the state.

This is where encryption earns its keep. A stolen laptop with an encrypted disk is a bad day and a police report. The same laptop without encryption can mean a federal filing, letters to every client on it, and a lot of explaining to people who trusted you.

## The IRS asks too

The IRS comes at the same requirement from its side. Each time you apply for or renew your PTIN, Form W-12 asks you to confirm that you know paid preparers are required by law to create and maintain a written information security plan. The form points to two free IRS publications:

- **Publication 5708** is a sample WISP written for small tax and accounting practices. It's the best place to start.
- **Publication 4557** is the IRS guide to safeguarding taxpayer data, with checklists for the office.

## What to get done before January

None of this takes a large budget. It takes a few evenings and some follow-through. If I were sitting in your office, this is the order I'd go in:

- [ ] Decide who your Qualified Individual is, and write the name down.
- [ ] Download Publication 5708 and fill it in with how your office actually works today, not how you wish it worked.
- [ ] Turn on MFA for email, tax software, the client portal, cloud storage, and any remote access tool.
- [ ] Check that every laptop and desktop has disk encryption on (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac).
- [ ] Stop taking tax documents by plain email, and point clients to a portal or another encrypted option.
- [ ] List everyone who has access to client data, including outside help, and remove accounts nobody uses.
- [ ] Ask your IT provider and software vendors what they do to protect your data, and get it in writing.
- [ ] Run one short staff session on phishing and fake sign-in prompts before the season starts.
- [ ] Do a test restore from your backup, so you know you can get files back.
- [ ] Write down who to call, and in what order, if a laptop goes missing or an account gets taken over.

Keep the finished plan somewhere you can find it, and look at it again when something changes: a new hire, new software, or a second location. A plan that lives in a drawer protects no one.
