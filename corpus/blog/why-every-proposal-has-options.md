# Why every neon1 proposal has options

A dental office manager gets a quote in mid-December: a new server, eight new workstations, and a three-year support contract, one price, about $40,000. The email ends with a nudge to sign before December 31 "for the tax break." The office's real problem is that the front desk computers are slow in the morning. She has two weeks, one number, and no way to tell which parts of it would fix that.

The nudge isn't made up. Since last year, the [Section 179 limit](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/working-families-tax-cuts-businesses) is much larger: businesses can elect to expense up to $2,500,000 of qualifying equipment and software placed in service during the tax year, up from $1,000,000, with the amount adjusted for inflation from 2026 on. That's real help for a business that needs the equipment. It's also why December inboxes fill up with "buy before the year ends" quotes, and a deduction only softens the cost of something you needed anyway. Your accountant can tell you whether it applies to you. Whether you need the server is a different question, and one price can't answer it.

That's why every neon1 proposal has options. When you ask me for one, you get a few, usually three, from the quickest fix to the fuller build, each with what it includes, what it leaves out, the hours I expect it to take, and the cost. You pick one, or none.

## One price hides the decision

A single quote asks you to decide yes or no on a package someone else designed. It doesn't show what you'd give up by spending less, or what you'd gain by spending more. If the number feels high, your only move is to haggle or walk away.

Options turn that into a real choice. You can see that the middle option adds automated backups and staff training, and decide whether those are worth the difference to you this year. You know your business, your budget, and your patience for change better than I do.

## The smallest option is often right

Many problems need less than people expect. The slow front desk might be a startup list full of programs nobody uses, a failing drive in one machine, or a backup job scheduled for 8 a.m. Sometimes the fix is a setting, a cleanup of old accounts, or a short checklist. I put that option in writing even when it means a smaller job for me, because it's often the right answer, and because you should know it exists before you pay for something bigger.

Each option also says what it won't do. If the quick fix leaves a risk in place, the proposal says so plainly.

## Deciding the budget first

Software teams have an idea called [appetite](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03): decide how much time a problem is worth before designing the solution, then shape the work to fit. I think about small business projects the same way. Three options at three sizes let you set the appetite, and I shape the work to match it, instead of the other way around. A deadline like December 31 is a fine reason to decide sooner. It isn't a reason to decide bigger.

## What a proposal looks like

Each proposal follows the same template:

- What you told me you need, in your words.
- Two to four options, each with what it includes, what it leaves out, hours estimated task by task, and any outside costs.
- What each of us will do, so nothing falls between us.
- How we'll know the work is done: the checks we'll run together at handover.

Once you approve an option, by signing or with a click in the 1NEON portal, that option becomes the agreed work. You can read the [blank proposal template](../templates/proposal.md) and a [filled-in example](../examples/proposal-options.md) for a fictional bakery with three options, and borrow either for your own vendors. Asking any vendor for two or three sizes of the same job is a fair request, and a good one will be glad to give them.
