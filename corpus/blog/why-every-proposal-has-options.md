# Why every neon1 proposal has options

The standard advice for hiring any kind of help is to get three quotes. It's good advice as far as it goes. Comparing prices keeps everyone honest, and a quote far above the others is worth a question.

But three quotes for the same job only answer one question: who will do this thing for the least money. They don't ask whether it's the right thing. If all three vendors heard "our computers are slow" and quoted a new server, you'll get three prices for a server, and no one will have told you that the slowness might be a backup job scheduled for 8 a.m.

So when someone asks me for a proposal, they don't get one price. They get a few options, usually three, from the quickest fix to the fuller build. Each one says what it includes, what it leaves out, the hours I expect it to take, and what it costs. You pick one, or none.

A single price hides the decision inside the package. If the number feels high, your only moves are to haggle or walk away. With options laid side by side, you can see that the middle one adds automated backups and staff training, and decide whether that's worth the difference to you this year. You know your business, your budget, and your patience for change better than I do. My job is to make the trade-offs visible, not to make them for you.

The smallest option matters most. A lot of problems need less than people expect: a setting nobody turned on, a cleanup of old accounts, a short checklist. I write that option down even when it means a smaller job for me, because it's often the right answer, and you should know it exists before you pay for something bigger. Every option also says what it won't fix, so a cheaper choice never hides a risk.

Software teams have a word for deciding the budget first. In *Shape Up*, Ryan Singer calls it [appetite](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.2-chapter-03): decide how much a problem is worth before designing the solution, then shape the work to fit. Three options at three sizes let you set the appetite, and I shape the work to match it.

This matters most in December. Since last year, the [Section 179 limit](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/working-families-tax-cuts-businesses) lets a business elect to expense up to $2,500,000 of qualifying equipment and software placed in service during the year, up from $1,000,000, and it's indexed for inflation from 2026. That's real help for a business that needs the equipment, and it's also why year-end quotes so often close with "sign before December 31." Your accountant can tell you whether the deduction applies. Whether you need the server is a separate question. A deadline is a fine reason to decide sooner. It isn't a reason to decide bigger.

You can read the [blank proposal template](../templates/proposal.md) I use and a [filled-in example](../examples/proposal-options.md) with three options for a fictional bakery. Borrow either for your own vendors. Asking anyone you hire for two or three sizes of the same job is a fair request, and a good vendor will be glad to answer it.

So keep getting three quotes. Just ask each vendor for three options too.
