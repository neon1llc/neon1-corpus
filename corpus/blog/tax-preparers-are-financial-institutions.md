# Tax preparers are financial institutions, as far as the FTC is concerned

If you prepare tax returns for a living, a federal rule written with banks in mind applies to your office. It doesn't matter whether you're a sole preparer working from home or a ten-person firm. The Federal Trade Commission names tax preparation services as an example of a "financial institution" under its Safeguards Rule, and it expects you to protect client information the way that rule describes.

This post is the short version of what the rule asks, in plain words, and what a small office can get done before tax season starts in late January.

## Why tax offices are on the list

Look at what sits in a typical client folder: names, Social Security numbers for a whole family, income, bank account and routing numbers for the refund, prior returns, and copies of driver's licenses. That's everything someone needs to file a fake return and take the refund, or to open accounts in a client's name. Criminals know this, so tax offices get targeted with phishing and fake "new client" emails, especially in the weeks before filing season.

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act requires financial institutions to protect their customers' personal information. Banks answer to their own regulators. For most other businesses that offer financial services to consumers, including tax preparers, the FTC enforces it through the Safeguards Rule, 16 CFR Part 314.

The FTC rewrote the rule in 2021 to spell out specific requirements, and most of them took effect on June 9, 2023. A later amendment added a duty to report certain breaches to the FTC, starting May 13, 2024.

## What the rule asks for, in plain words

The rule asks for a written information security program that fits your size and the sensitivity of what you hold. In the tax world this is usually called a WISP, for written information security plan. For a small office, it comes down to these pieces:

- **One person in charge.** The rule calls this person the Qualified Individual. It can be you, someone on staff, or an outside provider. If it's a provider, you still own the result and you name someone senior to oversee them.
- **Multi-factor authentication** for anyone who signs in to a system with client information. That means email, tax software, the client portal, cloud storage, and remote access, not only one of them.
- **Encryption** of client information when it travels outside your network and when it's stored, including on laptops and backup drives.
- **Access limited to what each job needs**, and a list of the systems, devices, and people that touch client data.
- **Security training for everyone**, kept current as new risks show up.
- **Contracts that require your service providers to protect the data**, and a periodic check that they do. Your IT person, your software vendors, and any outside bookkeeper count.
- **Secure disposal** of a former client's information no later than two years after you last used it, unless you have a legal or business reason to keep it.
- **A written plan for what happens when something goes wrong**, who decides what, and who gets told.

If you keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers, you can skip four of the formal pieces: the written risk assessment, penetration testing and scans, the written incident response plan, and the annual report to leadership. Everything else still applies, including MFA, encryption, and training. Count carefully before relying on this, because former clients count, and a practice that has been open for a while usually has more than its current client list suggests.

## The 30-day clock

Since May 13, 2024, if unencrypted information on 500 or more people is taken, you have to tell the FTC through a form on its website as soon as possible, and no later than 30 days after you find out. Encrypted data counts as unencrypted if the key was taken too. The FTC can publish these reports. New York's own breach notification law applies on top of that, with notices to the affected people and the state.

This is one more reason encryption matters for a small office. A stolen laptop with an encrypted disk is a bad day. The same laptop without encryption can become a federal report.

## The IRS asks too

The IRS comes at the same requirement from its side. When you apply for or renew your PTIN each year, Form W-12 asks you to confirm that you're aware paid preparers are required by law to create and maintain a written information security plan. The form points to two IRS publications, and both are free:

- **Publication 5708** is a sample WISP written for small tax and accounting practices. It's the best place to start.
- **Publication 4557** is the IRS guide to safeguarding taxpayer data, with checklists for the office.

## What to get done before January

None of this needs a large budget. For a small office, I'd work through it in this order:

- [ ] Decide who your Qualified Individual is and write the name down.
- [ ] Download Publication 5708 and fill it in with how your office actually works today, not how you wish it worked.
- [ ] Turn on MFA for email, tax software, the client portal, cloud storage, and any remote access tool.
- [ ] Check that every laptop and desktop has disk encryption on (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac).
- [ ] Stop taking tax documents by plain email, and point clients to a portal or another encrypted option.
- [ ] List everyone who has access to client data, including outside help, and remove accounts nobody uses.
- [ ] Ask your IT provider and software vendors what they do to protect your data, and get it in writing.
- [ ] Run one short staff session on phishing and fake sign-in prompts before the season starts.
- [ ] Do a test restore from your backup, so you know you can get files back.
- [ ] Write down who to call, and in what order, if a laptop goes missing or an account gets taken over.

Keep the filled-in plan somewhere you can find it, and look at it again when something changes, like a new hire, new software, or a second location.
