# Operating principles

A good engagement leaves you better able to run your own technology, without needing me for every small change. These are the habits I hold myself to so that happens. Each one says what I do, not what I hope for.

## Begin with the work

I ask the person who does the task to show me the last real example. I write down how often it happens, what goes wrong, and the workaround people already use. A request for "AI" sometimes turns out to need a clearer form or a shared folder, and that's a fine answer.

## Leave ownership with you

Accounts, billing, and recovery methods are yours, in your name. I get named, limited access that ends when the work does. The handover says who owns each account and how to leave each tool. If a tool makes it hard to export your data, I tell you before you buy it.

## Charge for responsibility and useful work

Scoping, testing, documentation, and training are part of the work, not extras. A price covers the result and the care that goes with it, not only the minutes spent changing settings.

## State uncertainty early

I keep five things apart: what we observed, what someone remembers, what we estimated, what a demo showed, and what we're proposing. Mixing them up is how projects end with surprises.

## Match promises to coverage

A system can run around the clock while I work by appointment. Every service says who responds when something fails and when. An automatic alert isn't a staffed response, and I won't sell it as one.

## Prefer the smallest solution that lasts

I check what you already pay for before adding anything new. Maintenance, vendor changes, access, and recovery all count in the decision. Free software you host yourself still costs someone's time.

## Make you an effective participant

I explain choices in terms of your task. Then I show the routine, you do it, and you fix one common mistake on purpose. If a tool would add work or watch your staff in ways they'd object to, that counts against it.

## Make failure recoverable

Before a change, I know how to undo it, how to get the data back, and how the business keeps going in the meantime. When a job needs faster response or deeper expertise than I can give, I help you find someone who can.

## Choosing engagements

A project is a good fit when the problem repeats, you can describe what success looks like, the data is yours to share, the work fits the calendar, and there's a manual way to keep going if something fails. The person who pays and the person who uses the system are often different people, and I talk with both.

I refer out continuous emergency support, safety-critical equipment, regulated professional judgment, specialist security assurance, and large migrations. A good referral is good service.

## Commercial basics

- Projects have a written scope and a fixed price by default, with the assumptions written down.
- Third-party subscriptions, hardware, and travel are separate, and I prefer that vendors bill you directly.
- No extra work or spending happens without your written approval.
- A signed agreement governs each engagement. These principles explain how I work. They don't replace it.

## Partnerships

When I work with another firm, the agreement names who sells, who invoices, who touches the data, who supports what, who owns the deliverables, and who carries insurance. If I'd receive a referral fee, you'll know. Working with an AWS Partner doesn't make neon1 one, and a certification I hold personally doesn't certify the business.
